A good bottle or jar label does three things: it makes the product easy to recognise on a shelf, it gives people the information they need, and it fits the container so well that it looks like part of the product. Getting there starts with measurements, not a mood board. This guide walks through measuring containers, choosing label shapes and materials, laying out content and preparing files your label printer can use without corrections.

## Start with the container

The container decides what the label can do. Before any design, get:

- the **container specification** or technical drawing from your supplier
- the **container itself**, filled if possible
- the **closure** you will use, such as cap, pump or dropper
- the **filling and labelling method**: by hand or by machine

### Know the label panel

Most containers have a defined **label panel**: an area that is straight or gently curved, between the shoulder and the base. Outside this area, the container curves, tapers or has ridges that a label cannot follow.

On your container drawing, find:

- the **label panel height**: the usable height between shoulder and base
- the **circumference** at the label panel
- any **tapering**: some bottles and jars are wider at the base than the shoulder

### Measure properly

If you do not have a drawing:

1. Measure the **usable height** of the label panel, leaving a margin from the shoulder curve and the base.
2. Measure the **circumference** with a flexible tape or a strip of paper.
3. Check for **taper** by measuring circumference at the top and bottom of the panel.
4. Note any **embossing, seams or ridges** on the container.

Your label printer can often provide a **template** for common containers.

## Choosing a label format

### Front and back labels

Two separate labels, one on the front and one on the back. This is common for wine, spirits, sauces and cosmetics. The gap between them can show the product or the container.

### Wrap-around labels

One label that wraps around most or all of the container. There is usually a small **overlap** or **gap** at the seam. Wrap-around labels give more space and a continuous design.

### Neck labels and collars

Small labels around the neck or shoulder, common on wine, spirits and craft drinks. They carry a logo, vintage or variant.

### Lid and top labels

Labels on jar lids or caps help customers identify products from above, useful on shelves, in fridges and in cupboards.

### Tamper-evident seals

Strip labels across the cap and container that tear when opened.

### Booklet and peel-back labels

Multi-layer labels that peel open to reveal extra information. Useful for multi-language products, supplements and small containers with lots of mandatory text.

### Shrink sleeves

Full-body film sleeves that shrink to fit. See our guide comparing shrink sleeves and pressure-sensitive labels.

## Choosing label materials

### Paper labels

- **Uncoated or textured papers** for wine, spirits, craft food and premium products. They feel tactile but are less resistant to water and oil.
- **Coated papers** for bright, sharp print at lower cost.

**Best for:** dry products and containers that will not get wet.

### Film labels

- **White film** (such as BOPP or PE) for durability and bright colours.
- **Clear film** for a "no-label" look.
- **Metallic film** for a reflective effect.
- **Squeezable films** for flexible bottles that are squeezed in use.

**Best for:** bathrooms, kitchens, fridges, oils and anything that will get wet or handled a lot.

### Adhesives

Adhesives must suit the container material, the surface temperature, any condensation and the product. Chilled and frozen products, oily containers and textured glass need specific adhesives. Tell your printer about the conditions.

## Laying out content

### Front label hierarchy

Customers decide quickly. Usually the front label should carry:

1. **Brand**
2. **Product name** or type
3. **Variant**, such as flavour, scent or strength
4. **Key benefit** or claim, briefly
5. **Net quantity**, where required on the front

Keep it simple. Everything else can go on the back.

### Back label content

Depending on the product: ingredients, allergens, nutrition or supplement facts, directions, warnings, storage, business name and address, country of origin, barcode, recycling information and a space for batch and date codes.

### Design for the curve

On a round container, only about a third of the circumference is visible at once. Keep the most important content within the **front third**, centred on the label panel. Elements near the edges of a wrap-around label will curve out of view.

### The seam

Keep text, barcodes and key graphics away from the **seam** where the label ends meet. A small overlap or gap is normal, so design the background to hide it, for example with a solid colour or a pattern that does not need to line up.

### Text size and legibility

Small labels have little space, but mandatory text still has minimum sizes, and customers still need to read it. Use a clear typeface, high contrast and sensible line spacing. Check legibility by printing the proof at actual size and wrapping it around the real container.

### Barcodes on curved containers

On small, tightly curved containers, rotate the barcode so the bars run horizontally (ladder orientation), which reduces distortion. See our barcode guide.

## Jars: special considerations

- **Short, wide jars** have less label height, so prioritise.
- **Lids** carry branding and variant information that customers see from above.
- **Glass jars** often show the product, so the label should frame rather than hide it.
- **Tapered jars** need **conical** label shapes, which your printer can calculate.

## Preparing print files

1. **Use the printer's die line** for the label shape, including bleed and safe areas.
2. **Extend backgrounds** into the bleed.
3. **Set up white underprint** for clear and metallic labels as a separate layer.
4. **Separate finishes** such as foil, embossing and varnish onto their own layers.
5. **Specify colours** in CMYK and spot references.
6. **Indicate the unwind direction** if labels will be applied by machine; your printer will advise.
7. **Leave space for date and batch codes** where your filler will print them.

## Testing

- **Print the proof** at actual size and wrap it around the real container.
- **Check the visible face** from a normal viewing distance.
- **Test durability:** water, fridge, oil, handling.
- **Check the seam** and edges for lifting or wrinkling.
- **Scan the barcode** on the container.

## Common label problems and fixes

- **Wrinkling:** the label is too large for the flat area, the container tapers, or the material is too stiff. Reduce the height, use a conical shape or choose a more conformable film.
- **Flagging** (edges lifting): wrong adhesive or a too-tight curve for the material.
- **Bubbles:** often from manual application or surface contamination.
- **Scuffing:** add a protective varnish or laminate.
- **Colour mismatch with the box:** proof both on their real materials.

## Label design checklist

Before you approve a bottle or jar label:

- The label fits the **usable label panel** with margins at the shoulder and base.
- Key content sits in the **front third** of a round container.
- Nothing important is on the **seam** or overlap.
- The **material and adhesive** suit the product and conditions.
- **White underprint** is set for clear or metallic labels.
- **Mandatory text** is complete and legible at actual size.
- The **barcode** scans on the container.
- There is space for **batch and date codes.**
- A print of the proof at actual size has been **tested on the real container.**

## Labels and boxes together

Many bottled and jarred products also need cartons or gift boxes. Designing labels and boxes together keeps colours, type and finishes consistent across different materials, and makes sure the box shows the label well when opened.
