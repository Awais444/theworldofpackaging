Selling in Canada means designing for two languages. Most mandatory information on consumer packaging must appear in both English and French, and that one rule shapes the whole back of the pack. This guide covers what usually has to appear on Canadian packaging for non-food consumer products, food and cosmetics, the extra rules for Quebec, and how to plan the space before design starts.

It is written for brand owners and designers. It is not legal advice, and your product may have rules we cannot cover here. Use it to prepare the right questions for your adviser, and check the official Government of Canada pages linked below.

## The bilingual rule in one paragraph

For most prepackaged consumer products sold in Canada, the mandatory information must be shown in English and French. There is one common exception: the name and principal place of business of the company responsible for the product (the "dealer") may be shown in either language. Information you add voluntarily, such as directions or marketing copy, does not have to be bilingual under the federal rules, although the Competition Bureau encourages it. Quebec adds its own French-language rules on top, covered below.

## Non-food consumer products: the Consumer Packaging and Labelling Act

The Consumer Packaging and Labelling Act and its Regulations cover most prepackaged non-food consumer products, such as household goods, candles and gift products. The Competition Bureau administers them for non-food products. Its guide lists three things that must be on the label:

1. **Product identity.** The common or generic name of the product, or its function, in English and French. Some words are already bilingual, such as "cologne".
2. **Net quantity.** In metric units, by volume, weight or count as the product requires, in both languages. A metric symbol such as "g" or "mL" counts as bilingual. It must be clear and prominent, in distinct contrast to other information on the label.
3. **Dealer name and principal place of business.** In English or French. The address must be complete enough for postal delivery, and a post office box on its own is not enough.

Product identity and net quantity normally belong on the principal display panel, the part of the label a shopper sees first. The Regulations also set minimum type heights for the net quantity, based on the size of that panel. Check the table in the Competition Bureau's guide for your pack size before you fix the layout.

Some products have their own rules on top of these, such as textiles, toys and products with hazard warnings. Check the specific guidance for your category.

## Food: CFIA and Health Canada

Prepackaged food is regulated under the Food and Drugs Act and the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) enforces the rules and publishes a labelling checklist for industry. Mandatory information on consumer prepackaged food must be bilingual, again with the dealer name and address allowed in either language.

The core items on most prepackaged food labels are:

- **Common name** of the food, on the principal display panel.
- **Net quantity** in metric units, on the principal display panel.
- **List of ingredients,** including food allergens, gluten sources and added sulphites, in the format the rules set.
- **Nutrition Facts table** for most prepackaged foods.
- **Name and principal place of business** of the responsible company.
- **Date marking and storage instructions** where needed, for example a "best before" date on foods with a durable life of 90 days or less.
- **Front-of-package nutrition symbol** where the food meets the thresholds (see below).

### The Nutrition Facts table is not the US panel

Canada's Nutrition Facts table has its own prescribed formats. CFIA's guidance says the format is set by regulation and there is no room for other languages inside it. There are bilingual formats, including simplified formats for small packages, and the format you can use depends on the size of the available display surface. A US Nutrition Facts panel cannot be reused unchanged on a Canadian pack.

### The front-of-package nutrition symbol

Since 1 January 2026, foods that meet Health Canada's thresholds for saturated fat, sugars or sodium must show a front-of-package nutrition symbol. It is a magnifying glass with "High in" and the nutrient in English and French, and "Health Canada / Santé Canada" underneath. In general the threshold is 15% of the Daily Value per reference amount, or 10% for foods with a reference amount of 30 g or 30 mL or less. Some foods are exempt, and there are restrictions on certain nutrient claims on packs that carry the symbol. Check Health Canada's guide for your product before you design the front panel, because the symbol takes space you need to plan for.

## Cosmetics: Health Canada

Cosmetics are regulated by Health Canada under the Food and Drugs Act and the Cosmetic Regulations. Health Canada's industry guide on labelling cosmetics is the best starting point. In short:

- **Bilingual labels,** except for the list of ingredients, which uses INCI names and does not need to be in both languages.
- **Ingredient list** on the outer label, using INCI names.
- **Product identity, net quantity and any warnings or cautions** in English and French.
- **Dealer name and principal place of business** in English or French.

Manufacturers and importers must also submit a Cosmetic Notification Form to Health Canada for each cosmetic sold in Canada, with the full ingredient list. That is a regulatory filing, not a label element, but your label and your notification should list the same ingredients.

Natural health products, such as many supplements, follow separate Health Canada rules and need a product licence. Do not use the cosmetic or food rules for them.

## Quebec: French comes first

Quebec's Charter of the French language goes further than the federal rules. On products sold in Quebec, inscriptions on the product, its packaging and accompanying documents must be in French. Another language can appear as well, but it cannot be more prominent than the French.

Since 1 June 2025, the rules on trademarks have changed. A registered trademark can still appear only in another language, but generic or descriptive words in it, such as the scent, flavour, colour or ingredients, must now also appear in French on the product or on something permanently attached to it. If a French version of the trademark is registered, the French version must be used. There is a transition period to 1 June 2027 for some products made before 1 June 2025. The Office québécois de la langue française (OQLF) publishes the guidance and examples.

In practice, if you sell in Quebec, plan for French at least as prominent as English everywhere on the pack, not only on the mandatory text.

## How to plan a bilingual Canadian pack

Bilingual text roughly doubles the information on the back of the pack. The brands that handle it best plan for it from the first concept, not at the end. What we do on Canadian projects:

1. **Get the final text in both languages before layout.** Ask your adviser or translator for approved English and French copy. French text often runs longer than English.
2. **Plan the principal display panel first.** Product identity and net quantity in both languages, plus the front-of-package symbol for food if it applies, all need space on the front.
3. **Choose a layout system.** Side-by-side columns, English on one panel and French on the next, or stacked text. Pick the one that keeps both languages equally readable.
4. **Check type sizes at actual size.** Print a proof at 100% and measure the net quantity height and the smallest text against the rules.
5. **Design one system for Canada and the US.** Many Canadian brands also sell in the US. A shared front panel with country-specific back panels usually works better than one compromise label.

We lay out the text you supply and check legibility at actual size. You, as the business selling the product, stay responsible for the content of the label, so have your adviser approve the final artwork before print.
