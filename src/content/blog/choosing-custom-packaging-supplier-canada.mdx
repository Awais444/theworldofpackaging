Search for custom packaging in Canada and you will find local box plants, online print shops, overseas factories and design studios, often with very similar websites. They are not interchangeable. This guide covers what is specific to buying custom boxes and labels as a Canadian brand: bilingual artwork, currency, delivery, imports and timing. For the general questions to ask any designer or printer, read our guide on [how to choose a packaging designer or printer](/blog/how-to-choose-packaging-designer-printer).

## The four kinds of supplier you will find

1. **Local manufacturers.** Corrugated and folding-carton plants across Ontario, Quebec, BC and Alberta. Strong for shipping boxes and high volumes. Many do not offer design, and few make rigid gift boxes.
2. **Online print shops.** Upload artwork, pick a size, pay by card. Very low minimums, often from one box. Limited sizes, materials and finishes, and you supply print-ready files.
3. **Overseas factories and brokers.** Wide range of rigid and luxury packaging at low unit prices, usually with higher minimums, longer lead times and import paperwork.
4. **Design studios with production.** Design and printing handled by one team. Fewer handovers, but check what they make themselves and what they outsource.

None of these is the right answer for every brand. A coffee roaster ordering 10,000 mailers has different needs from a skincare brand launching 500 gift boxes.

## Seven checks for a Canadian brand

### 1. Can they handle bilingual artwork?

Most mandatory label information in Canada must be in English and French, and Quebec has stricter French rules. Ask whether the supplier lays out both languages, checks type sizes at actual size, and has designed bilingual packs before. Ask to see one. Our [Canadian labelling guide](/blog/canadian-packaging-labelling-requirements) explains the rules.

### 2. Is the quote in Canadian dollars, and what does it include?

A quote in US dollars moves with the exchange rate between quote and invoice. Ask for CAD, or agree when the rate is fixed. Then check what is included: design, plates or dies, samples or proofs, packing, delivery to your door, and taxes.

### 3. Where is it made, and who pays for the import?

If boxes are made outside Canada, imported goods may be charged duty and GST or HST at the border, and someone has to clear customs. Ask who handles it and whether the price is delivered with duties paid. The Canada Border Services Agency explains how importing works for businesses. A cheaper unit price can disappear once freight, brokerage and taxes are added.

### 4. What is the real minimum order?

Minimums vary from one box at online print shops to tens of thousands at some carton plants. Ask for the minimum per design, not per order, and the price at two or three quantities. The step between 500 and 1,000 units is often bigger than people expect.

### 5. What do you approve before production?

Ask whether you get a digital proof, a 3D mockup, a printed proof or a physical sample, what each costs, and whether colours are matched to Pantone. Get the approval process in writing. Most print disputes come from an approval step nobody agreed.

### 6. How long, door to door?

Lead time on a quote often means production only. Ask for the date the boxes arrive at your warehouse or 3PL, including design rounds, proofing, production and shipping. Collapsible rigid boxes ship flat, which can shorten freight and lower its cost for rigid packaging coming from far away.

### 7. Can they show real work?

Ask for photos of produced packaging, not only renders, in a format close to yours. Check reviews on independent sites, and be careful with case studies that appear word for word on several pages of the same website.

## Red flags

- A price with no size, material or finish written next to it.
- No written proof or approval step.
- Reviews only on the supplier's own website.
- Pages that repeat the same text with only the product name changed.
- Pressure to pay in full before you have approved a proof.

## A simple way to compare quotes

Put two or three quotes side by side with the same specification: size, board or stock, print, finishes, inserts, quantity and delivery address. Convert everything to CAD delivered to your door. Then compare what you approve before production and the date the boxes actually arrive. The lowest unit price is rarely the lowest total cost.
