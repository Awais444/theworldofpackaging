"How much does packaging design cost?" is one of the first questions every brand asks, and one of the hardest to answer in a single number. Two quotes for what sounds like the same job can be very different, and both can be fair. The difference is almost always scope: what is being designed, how many versions, how much research and structural work, and how finished the files need to be.

This guide breaks a packaging design project into its parts, explains the pricing models agencies and freelancers use, and shows how to build a budget you can defend. We do not quote market price ranges here, because they vary so much by country, studio and scope that a number without context does more harm than good. Instead, you will be able to read any quote, including ours, and see exactly what you are paying for.

## What you are actually paying for

A packaging design fee is mostly time: the hours of skilled people needed to take your product from a brief to files that can be printed. Those hours fall into a few stages.

### 1. Discovery and research

Before any design, a good team needs to understand the product, the customer, the competition and the channel. This can be a short call and a questionnaire, or a full shelf audit, competitor review and customer interviews. The more research involved, the higher the cost, and the lower the risk of designing the wrong thing.

### 2. Strategy and brief

Turning research into a clear brief: positioning, the message hierarchy on pack, must-have information and design criteria. For small projects this is a page; for a new brand or a major redesign it can be a separate strategy project.

### 3. Concept design

The creative exploration. Most projects include a set number of initial **concepts** or **design directions**, usually presented on realistic mockups. More concepts mean more time. Two well-considered directions often serve a brand better than six rushed ones.

### 4. Development and revisions

One direction is chosen and refined. This stage is driven by **revision rounds**: the number of times you give feedback and the design is updated. Unlimited revisions sound attractive but usually signal either a very high fee or a process that has not been thought through.

### 5. Range extension

Applying the chosen design across every **SKU**: flavours, sizes, scents, shades. The first product takes the most work. Each additional variant usually costs much less, but a large range still adds up.

### 6. Structural design

If the project includes the box structure itself, such as a custom dieline, insert or opening mechanism, that is extra work for a structural designer and needs careful dieline checks. If you are using a standard box style from your manufacturer, this cost may be small or zero.

### 7. Artwork and pre-press

Turning approved designs into **print-ready files**: placing artwork on the dieline, setting up finish layers for foil or spot UV, checking colours, bleed and text sizes, and laying out mandatory text such as ingredients and barcodes. This is detailed, careful work and is often underestimated.

### 8. Mockups and imagery

3D renders for your website, marketplace listings or investor decks. Some studios include a few basic mockups; detailed product renders are often a separate line.

### 9. Project management

Meetings, coordination with printers and manufacturers, file management and approvals. Larger projects need more of it.

## The main pricing models

### Fixed project fee

The most common model. The studio estimates the hours for the agreed scope and quotes a fixed price. Extra work outside the scope, such as additional concepts, revision rounds or SKUs, is quoted separately.

**Good for:** most projects, because you know the cost upfront.
**Watch for:** a vague scope. If the quote does not say how many concepts, rounds and SKUs are included, ask.

### Hourly or day rate

You pay for the time spent. Common for freelancers and for small, open-ended tasks like artwork updates.

**Good for:** small jobs, ongoing support, or projects where the scope is genuinely unclear.
**Watch for:** budget creep. Agree an estimate and a cap.

### Per-SKU pricing

A base fee for the first design, then a fixed price per additional variant. Useful for large ranges.

**Good for:** brands with many flavours, sizes or shades.
**Watch for:** what counts as a new SKU versus a minor change.

### Packages or tiers

Pre-defined bundles such as "basic", "standard" and "premium", each with a set number of concepts, rounds and deliverables.

**Good for:** comparing options quickly.
**Watch for:** whether the tier actually matches what you need.

### Retainer

A monthly fee for a set amount of design time. Suits brands with frequent new products and updates.

### Design credited against manufacturing

Some packaging manufacturers with in-house design teams, including us, charge a design fee and then **credit it against your production order**. If you produce with them, design effectively costs less or nothing extra. If you take the files elsewhere, you pay the design fee.

**Good for:** brands that plan to manufacture with the same supplier.
**Watch for:** the terms: minimum order, timing and what happens if you change suppliers.

## What makes a project cost more

- **A new brand,** where identity and packaging are designed together.
- **More concepts** and **more revision rounds.**
- **More SKUs,** especially if each needs different imagery.
- **Custom structures** and inserts.
- **Photography or illustration** commissioned specifically for the pack.
- **Complex regulated content,** such as supplement panels, multi-language labels or pharmaceutical text.
- **Many stakeholders** in approval, which adds rounds and meetings.
- **Tight deadlines,** which may need more people working in parallel.
- **Multiple markets,** with versions for different regulations or languages.

## What makes it cost less

- **A clear, written brief** with references, mandatory text and constraints.
- **Existing brand guidelines,** so the identity does not need designing.
- **Standard box styles** with manufacturer dielines.
- **Fewer, better concepts.**
- **One decision-maker** for approvals.
- **A range system** designed upfront so variants are quick to add.
- **Realistic timelines.**

## Hidden costs to plan for

Design is only part of the packaging budget. Brands are often surprised by:

- **Printing plates and tooling,** such as cutting dies and foil or embossing blocks, which are usually one-off costs per design.
- **3D mockups** and proofs.
- **Colour proofs.**
- **Regulatory review** by an adviser.
- **Barcode licensing** from GS1.
- **Photography** once stock exists.
- **Shipping and duties** if packaging is produced abroad.

Ask your designer and manufacturer which of these are included in their quotes.

## How to compare quotes

When you have several quotes, put them side by side and check:

1. **Scope:** concepts, revision rounds, SKUs, structure, mockups, artwork and pre-press.
2. **Deliverables:** what files you receive, in what formats, and whether you own them.
3. **Process:** how many meetings, who presents, how feedback is handled.
4. **Timeline:** dates for each stage.
5. **Exclusions:** what is not included, such as printing, photography or regulatory review.
6. **Rights:** whether you own the final artwork and can use it with any printer.
7. **Production knowledge:** whether the team designs with real print processes in mind.

The cheapest quote is often the one with the smallest scope. The most expensive mistake is designing packaging that cannot be printed as intended, or that needs redesigning after the first run.

## Setting a realistic budget

A practical way to budget:

1. **List what you need:** number of products, sizes and variants, structure, mockups, markets.
2. **Decide what you can supply:** brand guidelines, photography, mandatory text, dielines.
3. **Prioritise:** which products launch first, and which can follow once the system exists.
4. **Ask for a scoped quote** with each item listed.
5. **Keep a contingency** for an extra revision round or a late SKU.
