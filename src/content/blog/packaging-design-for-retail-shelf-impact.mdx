On a retail shelf, your product competes with dozens of others for a few seconds of attention. Shoppers are busy, distracted and often shopping on autopilot. Packaging that wins on shelf gets noticed from a distance, is understood quickly and gives people a reason to pick it up. This guide explains how shoppers actually look at shelves and the practical design techniques that improve shelf impact, from blocking and hierarchy to structure, displays and testing.

## How shoppers look at shelves

Understanding shopper behaviour helps you design for it.

### They scan quickly

Shoppers often scan a shelf in seconds, looking for familiar brands or for the product type they need. They do not read every pack.

### They see from a distance first

Shoppers usually see a shelf from a few metres away before they step closer. At that distance, they notice **colours, shapes and large marks**, not small text.

### They look for cues

Shoppers use **cues** to navigate: brand colours, logos, product photos, shapes and category conventions. They search for what they know, or for what looks like the right type of product.

### They compare when close

Once they are close, shoppers compare a few options. Now **product name, variant, key benefits and price** matter.

### They pick up and check

Picking up a product is a strong sign of interest. Then the back of pack, quality cues such as weight and finish, and details like size or ingredients come into play.

## The three jobs of shelf packaging

Packaging needs to do three jobs, in order:

1. **Get noticed** from a distance.
2. **Be understood** in seconds.
3. **Be chosen** over alternatives.

Different design elements serve each job.

## 1. Getting noticed

### Distinctive colour

Colour is usually the first thing people see. A distinctive colour in your category makes your product easier to spot and remember. See our guide to colour psychology in packaging.

### Strong shapes and contrast

Simple, bold shapes and strong contrast stand out at distance. Fine detail and subtle tones disappear.

### Brand blocking

When several facings of your range sit side by side, shared colours and design elements create a **brand block**: a larger visual shape that is much more noticeable than one pack. Design your range so packs work together when lined up.

### Structure

Unusual shapes, such as a distinctive bottle, a pyramid box or a tall carton, stand out among standard shapes. Structure can be a powerful differentiator, but must still fit the shelf and the retailer's needs.

### Finishes

Metallic foil, spot gloss and textures catch light and attention. Use them to highlight key elements rather than everywhere.

## 2. Being understood

### Clear hierarchy

Once noticed, shoppers need to understand what the product is. Make the order of information obvious:

1. brand or product name
2. product type
3. variant
4. key benefit

### Product type in plain words

Make sure the pack clearly says what it is. A creative brand name is not enough on its own; add a descriptor such as "face oil", "granola" or "dog shampoo".

### Variant cues

For ranges, make variants easy to tell apart with colour, illustration and clear names. Shoppers should be able to find their flavour or scent without reading small text.

### Imagery

Product photos, ingredient images or illustrations can communicate quickly. Make them relevant and appealing, and keep them consistent across the range.

## 3. Being chosen

### A reason to buy

Give one clear, credible reason to choose your product: a key benefit, a unique ingredient, a format advantage or a value proposition. One strong reason works better than many weak ones.

### Quality cues

Weight, board stiffness, print quality and finishes all signal quality when a shopper picks up the product.

### Honest information

The back of pack should answer questions quickly: ingredients, how to use, size, certifications. Clear, honest information builds trust.

### Price-value fit

Packaging should look appropriate for the price. Packaging that looks more expensive than the price can make shoppers suspicious; packaging that looks cheaper can undermine a premium price.

## Designing for real shelf conditions

### Facings

Retailers often place several units of the same product side by side. Check how your design looks as a row of facings, not just as a single pack.

### Shelf height

Eye-level shelves are often seen most, but your product may be placed higher or lower. Check that key information is visible from above and below, and that the top of the pack works if seen from above.

### Shelf edge and price labels

Shelf edges, price labels and promotional tags can cover the bottom of packs. Keep key information above the lower part of the front panel.

### Lighting

Shop lighting can be harsh, warm or cool. Glossy finishes can glare; dark colours can look flat. Check samples under different lighting.

### Neighbours

Your product will sit beside competitors. Design and test with competitors around it, not on a plain background.

## Shelf-ready packaging and displays

### Shelf-ready packaging

**Shelf-ready packaging** (SRP), also called retail-ready packaging, is an outer case designed to go straight onto the shelf, usually with a tear-off front or top. It makes restocking faster for retailers and keeps products tidy. Many large retailers request or require it.

Design SRP to:

- **open easily** without tools
- **display the products** clearly once opened
- **carry branding** that adds to the shelf block
- **meet the retailer's specifications** for size and strength

### Counter and floor displays

**Point-of-sale displays** can lift sales for new or impulse products. Counter displays hold a small number of units near the till; floor displays create a standalone presence. Design them to match your packaging, hold stock securely and assemble easily.

## Online shelves

Many shoppers now see products on screens before or instead of shelves. The same principles apply:

- **thumbnails** need strong colour and simple shapes
- **product names** must be readable when zoomed
- **listing images** extend the pack's story

Design packaging that works both on shelf and online.

## Testing shelf impact

Do not judge shelf impact on a screen at full size. Test it:

1. **Print mockups** at actual size on the real material.
2. **Build a shelf:** use a real shelf or a simulation with competitor products.
3. **Time the search:** ask people to find your product and record how long it takes.
4. **Ask what they see:** what is the product, who is it for, what does it cost?
5. **Test at distance:** look from several metres away.
6. **Test facings and blocks:** line up several units.
7. **Check thumbnails** for online listings.

Small tests with a handful of people can reveal big problems.

## Working with retail buyers

Retail buyers decide whether your product gets on the shelf, where it goes and how much space it has. Packaging influences those decisions:

- **Show the range as a block** in your sales presentation, not just individual packs.
- **Explain how the packaging helps the retailer:** shelf-ready cases, efficient case sizes, clear barcodes and strong shelf impact.
- **Ask for the retailer's packaging guidelines** early and design to them, including case sizes, barcode positions and any shelf-ready requirements.
- **Provide samples** that match production quality.
- **Share your launch plan,** including marketing support, so buyers can see how you will drive shoppers to the shelf.
- **Follow up after launch** with photos of the product on shelf and any sales data, and ask what could work better.

## Common shelf impact mistakes

- **Designing in isolation** without competitors.
- **Too much text** on the front.
- **Unclear product type.**
- **Variants that look too similar.**
- **Low contrast** that disappears at distance.
- **Key information at the bottom,** hidden by shelf edges.
- **Copying the category leader.**
- **Ignoring facings** and brand blocking.
