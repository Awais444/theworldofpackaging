For a startup, packaging is often one of the biggest early investments and one of the most visible. It has to make a new, unknown brand look credible, explain the product to people who have never heard of you, survive shipping, meet legal requirements and fit a tight budget, usually before you know exactly how well the product will sell. This guide covers how startups can plan packaging sensibly: what to prioritise, how to brief, when to use stock or custom packaging, how to manage minimum orders and how to set up for growth.

## What packaging needs to do for a startup

Established brands can rely on recognition. Startups cannot. Your packaging has to do more work:

1. **Explain what the product is** in seconds.
2. **Make the brand look credible** enough for people to take a chance on it.
3. **Stand out** from established competitors.
4. **Protect the product** through shipping and handling.
5. **Meet legal requirements** for your product and markets.
6. **Fit your margins** and cash flow.

Keep these priorities in mind when making trade-offs.

## Decide where you will sell first

Your first channel shapes your packaging:

- **Direct-to-consumer online:** protection in transit, unboxing, listing images and thumbnails matter most.
- **Marketplaces such as Amazon:** prep requirements, size and weight tiers, and thumbnail clarity.
- **Independent retail and boutiques:** shelf presence, display, and practical needs like stacking and barcodes.
- **Large retail:** strict retailer guidelines, shelf-ready packaging, larger volumes.
- **Markets, events and pop-ups:** visual impact and quick explanation.

Design for your first channel, but avoid choices that make the next channel impossible.

## Brand before packaging

Packaging is easier, faster and cheaper to design when the brand basics are in place: a name, a logo, colours, typography and a clear positioning. If you do not have these yet, consider designing the **brand identity and packaging together**, so they are built for each other.

At minimum, be clear on:

- **who** the product is for
- **what problem** it solves or what desire it meets
- **why** it is different from alternatives
- **what tone** the brand should have

## Write a clear brief

A good brief saves time and money. Include:

- **Product details:** what it is, sizes, quantities, variants.
- **Target customer** and price point.
- **Channels** and markets.
- **Competitors** and references you like and dislike.
- **Mandatory content:** ingredients, warnings, nutrition or other required text.
- **Budget and timeline.**
- **Practical constraints:** filling, shipping, storage.

## Stock, semi-custom or custom?

### Stock packaging

Off-the-shelf bottles, jars, pouches, tins and boxes, decorated with your labels or stickers.

**Pros:** low minimums, fast, low cost.
**Cons:** less distinctive; competitors may use the same containers.

### Semi-custom

Stock containers with custom printed labels, sleeves or boxes. Or standard box styles, such as tuck end cartons or mailers, printed with your design and made to your size.

**Pros:** a good balance of distinctiveness, cost and minimums. This is where many startups start.
**Cons:** more setup than pure stock.

### Fully custom

Custom shapes, structures, moulds and materials.

**Pros:** maximum distinctiveness and fit.
**Cons:** higher setup costs, longer lead times, higher minimums.

**A common path:** start with stock or semi-custom packaging, prove demand, then invest in more custom packaging as volumes and confidence grow.

## Minimum order quantities and cash flow

Most packaging suppliers have **minimum order quantities (MOQs)** per design. MOQs affect:

- **cash tied up** in packaging stock
- **storage space**
- **risk** if the design or regulations change
- **unit price,** which usually falls as quantity rises

Questions to ask suppliers:

- What is the MOQ per design and per size?
- Can several designs share a production run?
- What are the one-off costs, such as plates, dies and tooling?
- What are the lead times for first orders and reorders?
- Can you hold stock or deliver in batches?

Digital printing has made short runs of labels, pouches and some boxes more practical, which helps startups test and iterate.

## Spend where it matters

With a limited budget, put money where customers notice it most:

- **The front of pack:** clear hierarchy and strong design.
- **Protection:** damaged products cost more than better packaging.
- **The first impression:** for D2C, inside printing or a good insert can matter more than outer finishes.

Save where customers notice less:

- **Too many finishes:** one well-chosen finish beats several.
- **Over-sized boxes:** right-sizing saves material and shipping.
- **Custom structures** you do not need yet.

## Plan for compliance early

Legal requirements apply to startups as much as to established brands. Depending on your product, you may need:

- ingredient lists and allergen information
- nutrition or supplement facts
- warnings
- net quantity statements
- business name and address
- barcodes for retail
- market-specific requirements

Get the mandatory text from a qualified adviser **before design**, so the layout is built around it. Our compliance guides, including UK labelling and US food labels, explain the main topics.

## Test before you scale

Before committing to large orders:

1. **Make physical samples** and check them with the real product.
2. **Ship samples** through your normal couriers.
3. **Show prototypes** to potential customers and retailers.
4. **Use 3D mockups** for crowdfunding, pre-orders and investor decks.
5. **Run a small first batch** if your supplier allows.

## Build a system for growth

Startups often add products quickly. Designing a **packaging system** from the start makes this easier:

- **Fixed positions** for brand, product name, variant and key information.
- **A colour or pattern code** for variants.
- **Templates** for new products.
- **Consistent materials and structures** where possible.

A system keeps your range coherent as it grows and makes each new product faster and cheaper to launch.

## A simple launch timeline

Packaging usually takes longer than founders expect. A typical sequence:

1. **Brand and brief:** finalise positioning, identity basics and mandatory text.
2. **Concepts:** explore design directions on mockups.
3. **Development:** refine one direction across all products.
4. **Artwork:** prepare print-ready files.
5. **Samples:** produce and test physical samples.
6. **Production:** print and make packaging.
7. **Shipping:** deliver packaging to your filler or warehouse.
8. **Filling and launch.**

Work backwards from your launch date and build in time for samples and corrections. Ask suppliers for realistic lead times, especially for first orders and international shipping.

## Common startup packaging mistakes

- **Designing before the brand is clear.**
- **Copying category leaders,** making the product look like a cheaper version.
- **Ignoring mandatory text** until the end.
- **Ordering too much** of a first design before proving demand.
- **Choosing finishes over protection.**
- **Forgetting shipping and storage costs.**
- **No plan for new products.**

## Working with suppliers as a startup

Suppliers deal with many startups, and the relationship matters. A few ways to make it work:

- **Be clear about volumes,** including realistic forecasts, so suppliers can advise on the right process and pricing.
- **Ask for advice early.** A good supplier can suggest structures, materials and print methods that save money.
- **Order samples** before committing to full production.
- **Consolidate suppliers** where it makes sense. Fewer suppliers means fewer handovers and less risk of mismatched colours.
- **Agree on approvals** and who signs off proofs.
- **Keep your files organised,** with master artwork and versions for each product.

## When to redesign

Startup packaging does not have to last forever. Plan a review when:

- you move into a **new channel**, such as from online to retail
- your **range grows** beyond the original system
- your **positioning** or price changes
- you reach **volumes** that justify more custom packaging
