A packaging design project can feel opaque from the outside. You send a brief, wait, see some designs, give feedback, and eventually boxes arrive. Understanding what happens at each stage, and what is needed from you, makes the process faster, cheaper and more likely to produce packaging that works. This guide walks through a typical packaging design process from first conversation to production, with what happens, what you receive and what you need to provide at each step.

Every studio works slightly differently, and small projects may combine or skip stages. But the underlying sequence is similar almost everywhere.

## Overview

A typical packaging design process has nine stages:

1. **Enquiry and scoping**
2. **Brief**
3. **Research and strategy**
4. **Concepts**
5. **Development**
6. **Range extension**
7. **Artwork and pre-press**
8. **Proofs and approval**
9. **Production and delivery**

Let us look at each.

## 1. Enquiry and scoping

The process starts with a conversation about what you need. The studio wants to understand the product, the number of SKUs, the channels, the timeline and the budget, so it can scope the work and quote accurately.

**What you provide:** a short description of the product and project, quantities, launch date, budget range and any existing brand assets.

**What you receive:** questions to clarify the scope, then a proposal or quote listing what is included, such as number of concepts, revision rounds, SKUs, structural work, mockups and file preparation, and what is not.

**Tip:** the more specific you are here, the more accurate the quote. If you are unsure about something, such as whether you need a custom structure, say so and ask for options.

## 2. Brief

Once the project is agreed, the brief turns your goals into clear instructions for the design team. A good brief covers:

- **the product:** what it is, sizes, variants, materials and containers
- **the customer:** who buys it and why
- **the market:** channels, competitors and price point
- **the brand:** positioning, personality, existing guidelines
- **objectives:** what the packaging must achieve
- **mandatory content:** all required text and marks
- **constraints:** budget, structures, materials, filling and shipping
- **references:** packaging you like and dislike, and why
- **timeline** and approval process

**What you provide:** as much of the above as possible, plus the product itself or its dimensions and any brand files.

**What you receive:** a written brief to approve, sometimes developed together in a workshop.

**Tip:** collect all mandatory text, such as ingredients, warnings and nutrition data, at this stage. Late changes to required text are one of the most common causes of delays.

## 3. Research and strategy

For larger projects, the studio researches the market before designing. That may include:

- **shelf audits:** photographing products in stores
- **online audits:** how competitors appear in search results and marketplaces
- **customer insight:** reviews, interviews or surveys
- **positioning:** where your product sits against competitors and what space it can own
- **message hierarchy:** what the pack must communicate first, second and third

For smaller projects, this may be a quick review of competitors and references.

**What you receive:** a summary of findings and recommendations, sometimes with a refined brief.

**Tip:** share any data you have, such as sales, customer feedback or retailer comments. It makes the research more useful.

## 4. Concepts

This is the creative exploration. The design team develops a small number of distinct **concepts** or **design directions**, each a different answer to the brief.

Concepts are usually presented as **realistic mockups**: the packaging in 3D, on a shelf, beside competitors, or in a customer's hand. Good presentations explain the thinking behind each direction, not just the visuals.

Structural options, such as box style, opening and inserts, may be explored at the same stage if the project includes structural design.

**What you receive:** typically two or three concepts.

**What you provide:** consolidated feedback and a choice of direction, or a combination of elements from different directions.

**Tip:** judge concepts against the brief and the shelf, not personal taste alone. Ask: would a customer notice this, understand it and want it?

## 5. Development

The chosen direction is refined. Details are resolved: typography, colour, imagery, hierarchy and layout on every panel. Back-of-pack content is laid out. Finishes are specified.

This stage is driven by **revision rounds**. Each round, you review the design and provide feedback; the team updates it.

**What you receive:** refined designs, usually as mockups and flat panels.

**What you provide:** clear, consolidated feedback for each round.

**Tip:** nominate one person to gather and consolidate feedback from your team. Conflicting comments from several people slow everything down.

## 6. Range extension

Once the master design is approved, it is applied across every product in the range: flavours, sizes, scents, shades. A system of fixed positions and colour or pattern codes makes this efficient.

**What you receive:** designs for every SKU.

**What you provide:** final product names, variant details and mandatory text for each SKU.

**Tip:** check each SKU's text carefully. Copy-and-paste errors between variants are common.

## 7. Artwork and pre-press

Approved designs are turned into **print-ready files**. This involves:

- placing artwork on the **correct dieline** for each structure and size
- setting **bleed, safe areas and orientation**
- preparing **colour** in CMYK and spot colours
- setting up **finish layers** such as foil, spot UV and embossing
- placing **barcodes** at the right size and position
- final **proofreading** and checks

**What you receive:** digital proofs of each artwork file for approval.

**What you provide:** careful checking and sign-off of every proof.

**Tip:** read every word on the proof. Approval usually means you accept responsibility for the content.

## 8. Proofs and approval

Before full production, proofs confirm that everything works:

- **3D mockups** check fit, opening and assembly
- **colour proofs** check colour on the real material
- **digital proofs** combine dieline, colours, copy and finishes

**What you receive:** a digital proof and a 3D mockup.

**What you provide:** testing with your real product, and approval or requested changes.

**Tip:** check the proof against real conditions: the product's size, how it ships and how it sits on a shelf.

## 9. Production and delivery

Once the proof is approved, packaging goes into production. The manufacturer prints, cuts, folds, wraps and assembles as needed, checks quality and packs the finished packaging for delivery.

**What you receive:** finished packaging, delivered to you, your filler or your warehouse, and often a set of final files for your records.

**What you provide:** delivery details and any import information.

**Tip:** keep approved proofs and production photos as references for future reorders.

## Typical deliverables

By the end of a full project, you would usually have:

- the approved **design** for every SKU
- **print-ready files** on dielines
- **editable master files** for future changes
- **mockups** for marketing and listings
- **proofs** and approved references
- the **finished packaging**, if production is included

Check your contract for **file ownership** and usage rights.

## How long it takes

Timelines vary widely. Factors include the number of SKUs, the amount of research, structural work, the number of revision rounds, how quickly feedback comes back, proof approvals, production lead times and shipping. Ask for a timeline with dates for each stage, and plan backwards from your launch date with some contingency.

## What slows projects down

- **Unclear or changing briefs**
- **Late mandatory text**
- **Many decision-makers**
- **Slow feedback**
- **Skipping the proof** and finding problems in production
- **Changing the product size** after the dieline is made

## Design and manufacturing together

When design and manufacturing are handled by separate companies, handovers between them can introduce delays and errors: a finish that cannot be produced, a colour that shifts, a structure that needs changes. Working with a studio that also manufactures, or involving your manufacturer early, keeps designs realistic and saves time.
