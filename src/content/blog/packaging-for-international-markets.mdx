Taking a product to a new country is exciting, and packaging is usually one of the first things that has to change. Rules on labelling, languages and units differ. Retailers have their own requirements. Colours and images can be read differently. And the journey from factory to shelf gets longer. This guide explains what typically changes when you package for international markets and how to design a packaging system that adapts without starting from scratch each time.

It is a planning guide, not legal advice. Requirements depend on your product and each market. Our compliance guides cover the UK, US and EU in more detail, and your importer or adviser should confirm specifics.

## What changes between markets

When you enter a new market, review these areas:

1. **Regulations:** what must appear on the label and how.
2. **Languages:** which languages are required or expected.
3. **Units and formats:** metric or imperial, date formats, decimal separators.
4. **Responsible business:** whose name and address must appear.
5. **Marks and symbols:** conformity marks, recycling symbols, warnings.
6. **Retailer requirements:** barcodes, shelf-ready packaging, case sizes.
7. **Culture:** colours, imagery, symbols, tone.
8. **Logistics:** shipping distance, climate, handling and freight cost.

## 1. Regulations

Labelling requirements vary by product type and country. Examples of what can differ:

- **mandatory information,** such as ingredients, allergens, nutrition, warnings and origin
- **formats,** such as nutrition panels and supplement or drug facts
- **minimum text sizes**
- **claims** that are allowed or banned
- **ingredient naming,** such as INCI names for cosmetics
- **registration or approval** before sale, such as label approvals for alcohol in the US or cosmetic notifications in the EU and UK

Collect the requirements for each market at the start of the project, and design space for them.

## 2. Languages

Many markets require mandatory information in their **official language or languages**. In the EU, for example, food information must be in a language easily understood by consumers in the member state where it is sold, and member states can require their official languages. Some countries require several languages, and some regions within countries have their own requirements.

### Approaches to multi-language packaging

- **Multi-language packs:** several languages on one pack, often for groups of neighbouring markets.
- **Market-specific versions:** separate packaging for each market or language group.
- **Stickers or over-labels:** applied by importers for small volumes, where allowed.
- **Booklet or peel-back labels:** extra space on small containers.
- **Leaflets or inserts:** for detailed information, where allowed.

### Translation

Use professional translators familiar with your category's terminology, and have native speakers proofread the final artwork. Machine translation of mandatory text is risky.

### Typography

Choose typefaces that support all required characters and scripts, and allow space for translations that run longer than the original.

## 3. Units and formats

- **Net quantity:** most countries use metric units. The US generally requires both metric and US customary units for many consumer products.
- **Fluid ounces:** US and imperial fluid ounces differ.
- **Dates:** day-month-year and month-day-year formats can be confused. Many brands use written month abbreviations or follow local formats.
- **Numbers:** some countries use a comma as the decimal separator.
- **Nutrition values:** per 100 g or ml in some markets, per serving in others.

## 4. Responsible business and addresses

Many markets require the name and address of a **business responsible for the product locally**, such as:

- an **importer** or **distributor**
- a **Responsible Person** for cosmetics in the EU or UK
- a **food business operator** in the EU

Plan for one or more addresses on pack, or market-specific versions.

## 5. Marks and symbols

- **Conformity marks** such as CE or UKCA for certain product types.
- **Recycling and disposal marks,** which vary by country and can be mandatory in some, such as specific sorting instructions.
- **Warning symbols** and pictograms.
- **Estimated quantity mark (℮)** in the EU and UK.
- **Certification logos,** such as organic, vegan or FSC, which have their own rules.

## 6. Retailer requirements

Retailers in different countries may have specific requirements:

- **barcodes:** GTINs from GS1 and correct symbol types
- **shelf-ready packaging** and case sizes
- **pallet configurations**
- **labelling** of outer cases
- **sustainability** requirements for materials

Ask your distributor or retail buyer for their packaging guidelines.

## 7. Culture

Colours, imagery and symbols do not mean the same thing everywhere.

- **Colours** carry different associations, for example for celebrations, mourning or luck.
- **Imagery** of people, animals, hand gestures or landmarks can be read differently.
- **Numbers** can carry positive or negative associations in some cultures.
- **Tone of voice** that feels friendly in one market can feel too informal in another.
- **Category expectations** differ: what looks premium in one country can look ordinary in another.

Research each market, review local competitors and test with local customers or partners.

## 8. Logistics

International shipping puts more stress on packaging:

- **Longer journeys** with more handling.
- **Climate:** heat, cold and humidity can affect materials, adhesives and products.
- **Freight costs:** sea and air freight are priced on weight and volume.
- **Storage** at ports and warehouses.

Practical steps:

- **Right-size** packaging to reduce freight volume.
- **Use collapsible rigid boxes,** which ship flat and are assembled at the destination.
- **Strengthen outer cartons** and use corner protection.
- **Choose materials and adhesives** suited to the climate.
- **Test with real shipments.**

## Designing one system for many markets

You do not need a completely new design for each country. A flexible system can serve many markets.

### Shared front, flexible back

Keep the **front of pack** consistent across markets: brand, product name, key visual and a short descriptor. Use **market-specific back panels** or labels for regulated information and languages.

### Modular layouts

Design panels as **modules** that can be swapped: an ingredient module, a nutrition module, an address module. This makes market versions faster and reduces errors.

### Icons and numbers

Use icons, numbers and universal symbols where they help, such as usage steps or storage instructions, to reduce the amount of text needing translation.

### Master files and version control

Keep a **master artwork** and clearly named versions for each market. Track changes carefully, because errors multiply across versions.

### Language groups

Group markets that share languages and requirements into **clusters**, each with one version of the pack.

## Budgeting for international packaging

Expanding into new markets adds packaging costs that are easy to overlook:

- **translation and proofreading** for each language
- **regulatory review** for each market
- **extra artwork versions** and the time to manage them
- **separate print runs** or stickering for market-specific versions
- **stronger export packaging** and protective materials
- **freight, duties and storage** in each market

Plan these costs into your market entry budget, and look for ways to share versions across markets where the rules allow.

## A market entry checklist

1. Confirm the **product's legal category** in the new market.
2. Collect **labelling requirements** with your importer or adviser.
3. Identify required **languages** and arrange translations.
4. Confirm **units, formats and addresses.**
5. Check required **marks and symbols.**
6. Get **retailer and distributor requirements.**
7. Review **cultural fit** of colours, images and tone.
8. Plan **logistics** and packaging protection.
9. Update **artwork** using the modular system.
10. Approve **physical samples** and test shipping.

## Common international packaging mistakes

- **Using home-market packaging** without checking local rules.
- **Machine-translated mandatory text.**
- **Too little space** for translations.
- **Wrong units or date formats.**
- **Missing local addresses** or responsible persons.
- **Ignoring cultural associations.**
- **Underestimating shipping stress** and freight costs.
- **No version control,** leading to the wrong artwork in the wrong market.
