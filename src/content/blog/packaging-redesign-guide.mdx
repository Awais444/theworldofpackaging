A packaging redesign can lift sales, cut costs and reposition a brand. It can also confuse loyal customers, upset retailers and leave you with warehouses of old stock. The difference usually comes down to two things: redesigning for the right reasons, and managing the project well. This guide covers both: how to tell when your packaging needs a redesign, how to decide between a refresh and a full rebrand, and a step-by-step plan for running the project from audit to rollout.

## When a redesign makes sense

Redesigning because the team is bored of the current look is rarely a good reason. Customers see your packaging far less often than you do, and familiarity is valuable. Good reasons usually come from a change in the business or the market.

### Your market has changed

New competitors have arrived, the category's visual language has moved on, or your packaging now looks like everyone else's. If shoppers cannot spot your product quickly on shelf or in a search result, the packaging is not doing its job.

### Your channels have changed

Packaging designed for a farmers' market may not work in a supermarket. Packaging designed for retail may not photograph well for e-commerce, or survive courier delivery. Moving into a new channel is one of the most common reasons for a redesign.

### Your range has grown without a system

Products added one at a time often end up with inconsistent layouts, colours and hierarchy. Customers struggle to navigate the range, and every new product needs a fresh design. A redesign can introduce a system that makes the range coherent and easier to extend.

### Your positioning or price has changed

If you have moved upmarket, packaging that looks budget will hold you back. If you have moved towards value, overly premium packaging may confuse customers.

### Costs or sustainability have become a problem

Rising material, freight or storage costs, or pressure from retailers and customers to reduce plastic and waste, can justify a redesign of structure and materials even if the graphics stay similar.

### Regulations have changed

New labelling requirements can force changes. These are often a good moment to review the whole pack rather than squeezing new text into an old layout.

## Refresh, evolution or rebrand?

Not every redesign is the same size. It helps to decide early which you need.

### Refresh

Small changes that keep the identity intact: updated typography, clearer hierarchy, new photography, refined colours, improved finishes. Customers may barely notice, but the pack works better.

### Evolution

A more noticeable update that keeps the key brand cues, such as the logo, main colour or shape, but changes much of the layout, system and imagery. Most redesigns fall here.

### Rebrand

A new identity: logo, name, colours, tone and often structure. Appropriate when the business has fundamentally changed, after a merger, or when the old brand has a problem.

The bigger the change, the higher the risk of losing recognition, and the more work is needed to bring customers, retailers and staff along.

## Protecting what customers recognise

Before changing anything, find out what your customers actually recognise. These are often called **brand assets** or **distinctive assets**: the colours, shapes, logo position, characters, patterns or words that people associate with your product.

Ways to identify them:

- **Ask customers** to describe your packaging from memory.
- **Show blurred or cropped images** of your pack and see what they recognise.
- **Check reviews and social media** for how customers refer to your product.
- **Look at your sales data** for products that perform well and what they share.

Keep the strongest assets unless there is a compelling reason to change them. Change everything else as needed.

## A step-by-step redesign plan

### Step 1: Audit

Start with an honest review of your current packaging:

- **Shelf and screen:** photograph your pack beside competitors in real stores and capture it in search results and marketplace listings.
- **Customer feedback:** reviews, complaints and comments.
- **Retailer feedback:** what buyers and store staff say.
- **Cost:** materials, print processes, finishes, sizes, freight and storage.
- **Compliance:** mandatory text that needs updating.
- **Sustainability:** materials, recyclability and waste.

### Step 2: Define the goals

Write down what the redesign must achieve, in measurable terms where possible. For example: "be recognisable at 2 metres", "fit our range on one shelf bay", "reduce unit cost by a set amount", "remove plastic windows". Agree which goals matter most if they conflict.

### Step 3: Write the brief

Turn the audit and goals into a clear brief: target customers, positioning, message hierarchy, mandatory content, brand assets to keep, constraints and budget. Include all the mandatory text and the number of SKUs.

### Step 4: Concepts

Ask for a small number of strong design directions, shown on realistic mockups **beside your current packaging** and **beside competitors**. This makes it easier to judge recognition and impact.

### Step 5: Test

Test concepts with real customers where you can. Simple methods work: show concepts on a simulated shelf and ask people to find your product, or ask which pack they would pick and why. Check online thumbnails too.

### Step 6: Develop and extend

Refine the chosen direction and build it into a system for the whole range. Create templates for future products.

### Step 7: Artwork and production

Prepare print-ready files for every SKU, sample the packaging and approve physical proofs before production. If structure or materials are changing, test them with the real product.

### Step 8: Rollout

Plan how the new packaging enters the market:

- **Stock:** use up old packaging before switching, or plan a write-off.
- **Retailers:** give buyers advance notice, new images and any updated product data. Some retailers have fixed windows for packaging changes.
- **Online:** update listings, product images and your website at the right time.
- **Customers:** consider a "new look, same product" message on pack or online to reassure loyal buyers.
- **Staff:** brief sales and customer service teams.

### Step 9: Measure

Compare sales, customer feedback and retailer response before and after the change. Keep notes for the next redesign.

## Managing the project

Redesigns often go wrong because of process rather than design. A few principles help:

- **Name one decision-maker.** Too many approvers lead to endless rounds and compromise designs.
- **Agree the feedback format.** Consolidated, written feedback is faster and clearer.
- **Lock mandatory content early.** Late changes to ingredients or regulatory text cause delays.
- **Involve your manufacturer early.** Structural and material changes need production input.
- **Plan the timeline backwards** from the launch date, including stock run-out and retailer deadlines.

## Common redesign mistakes

- **Changing everything at once,** including the logo, colour and structure, without testing recognition.
- **Designing in isolation,** without seeing the new pack on a real shelf or in search results.
- **Following category trends** so closely that the new pack looks like every competitor's.
- **Forgetting the back of pack,** leaving mandatory text squeezed into a new layout.
- **Ignoring production,** choosing finishes or structures that push unit costs beyond the budget.
- **No rollout plan,** leaving retailers with mixed old and new stock on shelf and outdated images online.
- **Skipping the physical sample,** so colour shifts, finish problems or structural issues only appear after thousands of boxes have been printed.
- **Measuring too soon.** Sales often dip briefly while customers adjust; judge results over a few months, not a few days.
