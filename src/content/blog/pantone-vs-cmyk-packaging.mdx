Colour is often the most recognisable part of a brand's packaging, and one of the hardest things to get right in print. A brand blue that looks perfect on screen can come back dull on the box, different on the label and different again on the shipping carton. Choosing between CMYK and Pantone spot colours, and understanding how materials change colour, is the key to consistency. This guide explains both systems, when to use each, and how to manage colour across all your packaging.

## How CMYK works

**CMYK** stands for cyan, magenta, yellow and key (black). These are the four **process colours** used in most commercial printing. Colours are created by printing tiny dots of each ink in different sizes and combinations. Up close you can see the dots; from a normal viewing distance, the eye blends them into a single colour.

**Strengths of CMYK:**

- **Full-colour images:** photographs, illustrations and gradients need CMYK.
- **Efficiency:** many colours from just four inks, which keeps costs down for designs with lots of colours.
- **Flexibility:** you can add new colours to the design without adding inks.

**Limitations of CMYK:**

- **Limited gamut:** CMYK cannot reproduce many bright, saturated colours, such as vivid oranges, greens, blues and purples, or metallics and fluorescents.
- **Variation:** because colours are built from dots, small changes in ink density, registration and press conditions shift the result. The same CMYK values can look different between print runs or printers.
- **Flat colour quality:** large areas of flat colour can look slightly textured or uneven compared with a solid ink.

## How Pantone spot colours work

A **spot colour** is a premixed ink printed as a solid colour, rather than built from dots. The **Pantone Matching System** is the most widely used reference for spot colours, with each colour given a number and a formula.

**Strengths of Pantone spot colours:**

- **Consistency:** the same premixed ink gives more consistent results between runs and suppliers.
- **Vibrancy:** many colours outside the CMYK range can be matched with a spot ink.
- **Special inks:** metallics, fluorescents and pastels are available as spot colours.
- **Clean flat areas:** solid ink gives smooth, even areas of colour.

**Limitations:**

- **Cost:** each spot colour usually needs its own ink and plate, which adds setup cost.
- **Not for images:** photographs still need CMYK.
- **Press capacity:** presses have a limited number of ink units, which limits how many spot colours you can add to CMYK in one pass.

## When to use each

### Use CMYK when

- your design includes **photographs or complex illustrations**
- it has **many colours** with no single critical brand colour
- the **run is small** and setup costs matter
- your brand colours **sit comfortably inside the CMYK gamut**

### Use Pantone spot colours when

- you have a **signature brand colour** that must look the same everywhere
- the colour is **outside the CMYK gamut**
- you need **metallic, fluorescent or pastel** effects
- your design uses **large flat areas** of one or two colours
- you print **across several suppliers** and need a shared reference

### Use both

Many packaging designs combine **CMYK for images** with **one or two spot colours** for the brand colour or a metallic accent. This gives full-colour imagery and an exact brand colour.

## How materials change colour

Even with the right inks, the **substrate** you print on changes how colour looks.

### Coated white board

Smooth, coated white board such as SBS gives the brightest, most accurate colours, because ink sits on the surface and the white reflects light.

### Uncoated board and paper

Uncoated materials absorb more ink, so colours look **darker, softer and less saturated**. Dot gain, the spreading of ink dots, is higher. Pantone provides separate references for coated (C) and uncoated (U) paper for this reason.

### Kraft and brown board

Brown kraft board shows through the ink, so colours shift towards brown and look much darker. White ink under colours can help, but it adds cost and changes the look. If kraft is part of your brand, design colours specifically for it.

### Corrugated board

Printing directly on corrugated board, usually by flexography, gives less detail and lower colour accuracy than printing on coated paper. Many premium mailers are made by printing on coated paper, which is then laminated onto corrugated board.

### Films and foils

Clear films need white underprint to keep colours visible. Metallic films change colours, making them look metallic unless white is printed underneath.

### Rigid box wrap papers

Rigid boxes are wrapped in printed paper, which can be coated, uncoated, textured or specialty stock. Each changes colour appearance. Linen and textured papers also affect how light reflects.

## Finishes change colour too

- **Gloss lamination** deepens and brightens colours.
- **Matte lamination** softens and slightly lightens them.
- **Soft-touch lamination** can mute colours noticeably, especially darks.
- **Varnishes** can shift colours slightly.

Always proof colours with the intended finish.

## Screens are not proofs

Screens display colour in **RGB**, using light, while print uses ink on a surface. Some colours visible on screen cannot be printed at all. Screen brightness and calibration also vary. Never approve packaging colour from a screen alone.

## Managing colour across your packaging

### 1. Define colour references

For each brand colour, define:

- a **Pantone reference** for coated and uncoated materials
- **CMYK values** for process printing, ideally tested on your main substrates
- **RGB and HEX values** for screens

Record which reference applies to which material.

### 2. Accept that some variation is unavoidable

A colour will never look exactly the same on a coated carton, a kraft mailer and a clear label. The goal is a **consistent family** that customers recognise, not mathematical identity.

### 3. Use physical references

Use printed colour guides and keep **approved boxes** from previous runs as references for future production.

### 4. Proof on the real material

Ask for **colour proofs or press proofs** on the actual substrate with the actual finish. For important launches, attend a press check or review photos from the first run.

### 5. Keep suppliers consistent

Using the same printer and process for each packaging type helps keep colour consistent between runs.

### 6. Communicate clearly

Give your printer the colour references, the approved proof and any tolerances. Agree how colour will be checked before production.

## Common colour mistakes on packaging

- **Approving colour on screen.** The printed result will almost always differ.
- **Using one CMYK value for every material.** A recipe tuned for coated white board will look wrong on kraft or uncoated stock.
- **Choosing a Pantone colour from the coated guide for an uncoated job.** Use the uncoated (U) reference for uncoated materials.
- **Building small text from four colours.** Fine text in rich black or CMYK mixes can look blurred if the plates are slightly out of register.
- **Too many spot colours.** Each one adds setup cost and can exceed the number of ink units on the press.
- **Forgetting the finish.** Soft-touch, matte and gloss laminates all shift colour after printing.
- **No approved reference.** Without a signed-off proof or colour reference, there is nothing objective to compare later runs against.

## A practical workflow

For most packaging projects, this approach works well:

1. **Choose brand colours** with print in mind, ideally from a Pantone guide viewed under good light.
2. **Check whether each colour is in the CMYK gamut** for your main materials.
3. **Decide per job** whether to print each colour as CMYK or as a spot colour.
4. **Set up files** with named spot colours and CMYK values, and no RGB.
5. **Request a physical proof** on the real material and finish.
6. **Sign off a colour reference** and keep it for future runs.
