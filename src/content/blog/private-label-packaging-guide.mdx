Private label is one of the fastest ways to launch a product brand. Instead of developing a formula or product from scratch, you work with a manufacturer that already makes it, and sell it under your own name. The catch is that other brands may be selling something very similar, from the same factory, in the same stock bottle. Packaging is often what decides whether your private label product looks like a real brand or just another version of the same thing. This guide covers how private label packaging works, how to work with manufacturers, how to stand out, how to handle compliance and how to build a range that can grow.

## What private label means

**Private label** (also called white label in some industries) means a product made by one company and sold under another company's brand. Common models include:

- **Stock formulas or products:** the manufacturer offers ready-made products, and you choose from their catalogue.
- **Modified formulas:** you adjust a stock product, for example changing the scent, flavour or a key ingredient.
- **Custom formulas:** the manufacturer develops a product to your specification.

Private label is common in skincare, cosmetics, supplements, food and drink, pet products, candles, cleaning products and more. Retailers also sell private label products under their own store brands.

## Who does what

In private label, responsibilities are shared between you and your manufacturer. Typically:

- **The manufacturer** makes the product, fills it into containers and may source containers.
- **You** own the brand, and usually the packaging design and marketing.
- **Packaging** may be supplied by the manufacturer, by you directly, or by a packaging supplier who delivers to the manufacturer.

Agree clearly in writing:

- who supplies **containers, closures, labels and boxes**
- who is responsible for **regulatory compliance** of the product and its labelling
- who holds the **product documentation**, such as safety assessments or specifications
- who applies **labels** and packs products
- what **quality checks** happen before delivery

In many markets, the business whose name is on the label is treated as responsible for the product. Check this with your adviser.

## Working with your manufacturer's requirements

Your packaging must fit the manufacturer's processes:

- **Containers:** sizes, neck finishes and materials they can fill.
- **Filling lines:** whether containers must be a particular shape or size to run on their equipment.
- **Labelling:** whether labels are applied by hand or machine, the roll specifications needed and the label position.
- **Packing:** whether products go into cartons on the line or by hand, and how they are packed for shipping.
- **Coding:** where batch numbers and dates are printed.
- **Minimums and lead times** for each component.

Ask your manufacturer for their **specifications and templates** before you start designing.

## Stock containers with custom decoration

Many private label brands start with the manufacturer's **stock containers** and add custom **labels, sleeves and boxes**. This keeps minimums low and launch times short.

The challenge is that competitors may use exactly the same containers. Ways to make stock containers look distinctive:

- **Custom labels** with strong design, premium materials or unusual shapes.
- **Different closures,** such as a wooden cap, a coloured pump or a metallic collar.
- **Decorated containers,** such as coloured or frosted finishes, screen printing or shrink sleeves.
- **Custom outer boxes** that create a strong shelf and unboxing impression.
- **Neck labels, seals and tags** for extra detail.

As volumes grow, you can move to **custom containers** for even more distinctiveness.

## Standing out from other brands on the same product

If several brands sell the same base product, your packaging needs to work harder. Differentiation can come from:

### Positioning

Decide who the product is for and why it is different. Is it for sensitive skin, active lifestyles, a particular age group, a specific ritual? Clear positioning guides every design decision.

### Design system

A distinctive colour, pattern, illustration style or typography creates recognition. Apply it consistently across every product.

### Information

Many private label products have similar claims. Clear, honest, well-structured information, such as how to use the product, what is in it and who it suits, can stand out more than louder claims.

### Structure and finishes

A rigid gift box, a soft-touch carton or a clever insert can make a familiar product feel new.

### Unboxing

For online sales, a thoughtful unboxing experience creates a memorable first impression.

### Range architecture

A well-organised range, with clear names and colour codes, helps customers choose and buy more.

## Compliance in private label

Even if the manufacturer makes the product, labelling usually needs to reflect your brand as the business responsible. Depending on the product and market, labels may need:

- **product identity** and description
- **ingredients** or composition, using the correct naming system
- **net quantity**
- **business name and address** of the responsible business
- **warnings** and directions
- **batch numbers** and dates
- **nutrition, supplement facts or other panels**
- **country of origin**
- **barcodes**

Ask the manufacturer for the **approved product information**, such as ingredient lists and specifications, and have your label text reviewed by a qualified adviser. ## Artwork ownership and supplier changes

Private label brands sometimes change manufacturers as they grow. Make sure you:

- **own the artwork files** for your packaging
- have **editable master files**, not just print PDFs
- keep **dielines and specifications** for each component
- understand whether any **container moulds or tooling** belong to you or the supplier

This makes it easier to switch manufacturers or packaging suppliers without starting again.

## Building a private label range

Most private label brands grow by adding products. Plan for this from the start:

1. **Create a design system** with fixed positions for brand, product name, variant and key information.
2. **Use colour or pattern codes** for product types or variants.
3. **Standardise containers and sizes** where possible, to simplify sourcing and keep the range coherent.
4. **Build templates** for labels and boxes, so new products can be added quickly.
5. **Document rules** for typography, colours and imagery.

## Costs and minimums

Private label packaging costs include:

- **design** of the brand and packaging
- **containers and closures**
- **labels, sleeves and boxes**
- **setup costs** such as plates, dies and tooling
- **filling and labelling** by the manufacturer
- **samples** and proofs
- **shipping** of packaging to the manufacturer

Ask each supplier about **minimum order quantities** and how they affect unit prices. Balancing minimums across containers, labels and boxes avoids being stuck with large stocks of one component.

## A private label packaging checklist

- Responsibilities agreed with the manufacturer in writing.
- Manufacturer's container, filling and labelling specifications collected.
- Positioning and design system defined.
- Distinctive elements chosen beyond the logo.
- Mandatory label text from approved product information and reviewed by an adviser.
- Artwork ownership confirmed.
- Templates built for future products.
- Physical samples tested on the manufacturer's line.

## Private label for retailers

Retailers developing their own store brands face similar questions at a larger scale. Their private label packaging often needs a **tiered system**, such as value, standard and premium lines, each with its own look but a shared structure, so customers understand the difference at a glance.

## Common private label packaging mistakes

- **Changing only the logo** on a manufacturer's generic design.
- **Ignoring the manufacturer's specifications** until artwork is finished.
- **Assuming the manufacturer handles compliance** without confirming it.
- **Not owning the artwork files.**
- **Unbalanced minimums,** with too many labels and too few boxes, or the reverse.
- **No system** for adding products.
