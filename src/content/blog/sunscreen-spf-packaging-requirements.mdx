Sunscreen packaging carries more required information than almost any other skincare product, and the rules depend heavily on where you sell. In the United States, sunscreen is an over-the-counter drug. In the European Union and the United Kingdom, it is a cosmetic. That single difference changes the label format, the claims you can make and the testing behind them. This guide explains the main requirements in each market and how to design SPF packaging that is clear and compliant.

It is general guidance, not legal or regulatory advice. Sunscreen rules are complex and have been changing, especially in the US. Use the official sources linked below, and have your label and claims reviewed by a qualified adviser with access to your test data.

## United States: sunscreen as an OTC drug

In the US, sunscreens are regulated by the FDA as **nonprescription (over-the-counter) drugs**, because they make a drug claim: protecting against sunburn and, for broad spectrum products, reducing the risk of skin cancer and early skin aging. The labelling and testing rules are set out in 21 CFR 201.327, alongside the general OTC drug labelling rules.

The regulatory framework for OTC sunscreens has been updated in recent years through the OTC monograph reform process, and the FDA has proposed further changes. Check the FDA's current position before finalising artwork.

### The Drug Facts panel

US sunscreens must carry a **Drug Facts** panel in the standard format set out in 21 CFR 201.66. It contains, in order:

- **Active ingredient(s)** and their concentrations
- **Purpose**, such as "Sunscreen"
- **Uses**
- **Warnings**, including "For external use only" and specific warnings for sunscreens
- **Directions**
- **Other information**, such as storage
- **Inactive ingredients**
- **Questions or comments?**, with a phone number (optional but common)

The panel has strict rules on headings, bullets, type sizes, line spacing and order. There are modified formats for small packages.

### SPF and broad spectrum

- **SPF** must be shown on the principal display panel, based on testing under FDA methods.
- **"Broad Spectrum"** may only be claimed if the product passes the FDA's broad spectrum test. Broad spectrum products with SPF 15 or higher can claim to reduce the risk of skin cancer and early skin aging when used as directed with other sun protection measures.
- Products that are not broad spectrum, or have SPF below 15, must carry a specific warning that they have not been shown to help prevent skin cancer or early skin aging.

### Water resistance and banned terms

- **Water resistance** can be claimed only for **40 minutes** or **80 minutes**, based on testing.
- Terms such as **"waterproof"**, **"sweatproof"** and **"sunblock"** are not allowed, because they overstate effectiveness.
- Claims of instant protection or protection for more than two hours without reapplication are restricted unless supported by data and FDA rules.

### Principal display panel

The front of a US sunscreen usually shows the product name, SPF value, "Broad Spectrum" (if applicable), water resistance (if applicable) and the net quantity. The FDA specifies how SPF and broad spectrum statements are presented, including their prominence relative to other text.

## European Union: sunscreen as a cosmetic

In the EU, sunscreens are cosmetic products under Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009. That means they follow the general cosmetic labelling rules: Responsible Person, nominal content, durability or PAO, precautions, batch number, function and ingredients (see our cosmetic labelling guide). UV filters must be on the list of permitted UV filters in Annex VI of the Regulation, within the concentration limits.

### The Commission Recommendation

The European Commission's **Recommendation 2006/647/EC** sets out how sunscreen efficacy and claims should be presented. Although it is a recommendation rather than a binding regulation, it is widely followed and is used by authorities to assess whether claims are misleading. Its main points are:

- **Minimum protection:** sunscreens should provide UVA protection at least one third of the labelled SPF, and a minimum critical wavelength.
- **UVA symbol:** products meeting the UVA standard can show the letters "UVA" in a circle.
- **Protection categories:** SPF values should be shown with a category: **low** (6 to 10), **medium** (15 to 25), **high** (30 to 50) and **very high** (50+).
- **Prohibited claims:** claims such as "100% protection", "total protection" or "sunblock", or that no reapplication is needed, should not be made.
- **Warnings and instructions:** labels should include advice such as not staying too long in the sun, keeping babies and young children out of direct sunlight, reapplying frequently, and applying enough product.

### Testing

SPF, UVA protection and water resistance claims should be based on recognised test methods. Many brands use ISO standards for these tests. Keep test reports on file; your Responsible Person will need them for the product information file.

## United Kingdom

In Great Britain, sunscreens are cosmetics under the UK's cosmetics rules, which are based on the EU Regulation. In practice, UK brands follow the same labelling approach as the EU, including the Commission Recommendation's categories and UVA symbol, with a **UK Responsible Person** on the label and notification on SCPN. Check for any UK-specific changes, as rules may diverge.

## Designing SPF packaging

Sunscreen packaging has to communicate protection quickly at the shelf, while carrying a lot of mandatory information. Some principles we use:

### Plan the mandatory text first

Write the full label text for each market before design. For the US, that means a full Drug Facts panel; for the EU and UK, warnings, instructions and the ingredient list. Measure how much space each needs at the minimum size.

### Make SPF unmistakable

The SPF value and, where applicable, broad spectrum or UVA statements are the most important pieces of information for shoppers. Give them a fixed, prominent position on every product in the range, and use a colour or numbering system to separate SPF levels.

### One range, different markets

If you sell in both the US and Europe, you will almost always need different labels, because the US Drug Facts format and EU cosmetic format are not compatible on one panel. A shared front design with market-specific back panels is a common approach.

### Tubes and bottles

Sunscreen often comes in tubes and bottles with curved surfaces. Test the Drug Facts panel or warnings on the real container. On tubes, remember the crimp area at the end and the cap at the other.

### Durability in heat

Sunscreen packs live on beaches and in bags. Choose label materials and coatings that resist sunscreen oils, water and abrasion, and check print durability before production.

## Common sunscreen label mistakes

- **Unsupported claims.** Using "broad spectrum", "water resistant" or a UVA logo without test data behind them.
- **Banned words.** "Sunblock", "waterproof" or "sweatproof" on US packs, or "total protection" in the EU.
- **Wrong SPF presentation.** Showing an SPF number without the EU protection category, or making the SPF less prominent than the brand's marketing claims on a US pack.
- **Missing warnings.** The US skin cancer and skin aging alert for non-broad-spectrum products, or the EU recommendation's usage advice.
- **One label for every market.** Trying to fit a US Drug Facts panel and EU cosmetic labelling on one pack, which usually satisfies neither.
- **Squeezed Drug Facts.** Reducing type size or spacing below the minimums to fit a small tube.
- **Outdated active ingredients.** Using UV filters that are not permitted, or above the permitted concentration, in a given market. This is a formula issue, but it affects the label.

## Planning a sunscreen launch

Sunscreen launches often run late because testing, label approval and packaging production are planned in sequence rather than together. A more reliable order is:

1. **Confirm target markets** and whether the product is a drug or a cosmetic in each.
2. **Commission testing** for SPF, UVA and water resistance as early as possible.
3. **Draft label text** in parallel, with placeholders for tested values.
4. **Design the packaging** with the mandatory content planned in, and fix the brand system.
5. **Finalise values** when test results arrive, and have the adviser review.
6. **Print and produce** packaging, allowing time for proof approval.
