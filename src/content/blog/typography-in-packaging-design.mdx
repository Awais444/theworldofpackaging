Typography does more work on packaging than almost anywhere else. In a few square centimetres, it has to express the brand's personality, make the product name instantly readable, separate variants, and carry legally required text at tiny sizes, all on curved, textured or shiny surfaces under shop lights. This guide covers how to choose typefaces for packaging, build hierarchy, keep text legible, handle multiple languages and avoid common print problems.

## The two jobs of packaging typography

Packaging type has two distinct jobs:

1. **Expression:** the brand name, product name and key messages, which carry personality and help the product stand out.
2. **Information:** ingredients, directions, warnings, nutrition and other details, which must be clear, legible and often meet legal requirements.

These jobs often need different typefaces, or at least different weights and sizes. Trying to make one typeface do everything can compromise both.

## Choosing typefaces

### Display typefaces

Display typefaces are used at larger sizes for brand and product names. They can be distinctive: serif, sans serif, script, geometric, condensed or custom lettering.

When choosing, consider:

- **personality:** classic, modern, playful, technical, crafted, luxurious
- **category fit:** what is expected, and where you want to differ
- **legibility** at the size and distance it will be seen
- **distinctiveness** compared with competitors

### Text typefaces

Text typefaces are used for information. They need to be:

- **clear at small sizes,** with open letter shapes and generous x-heights
- **distinct** in characters that are easily confused, such as 1, l and I, or 0 and O
- **available in several weights,** for hierarchy within information
- **robust in print,** without hairline strokes that break up

Many packs pair a distinctive display face with a clear sans serif for information.

### How many typefaces?

Usually **one or two typefaces** with a limited set of weights is enough. Each additional typeface makes the pack look less consistent. If you need more variety, use weights, sizes and styles within the same family.

## Building hierarchy

Hierarchy tells the customer what to read first. On the front of pack, a typical order is:

1. **Brand** or **product name**, whichever leads
2. **Product type or descriptor**
3. **Variant**, such as flavour, scent or strength
4. **Key benefit** or claim
5. **Net quantity** and other required front-of-pack items

Use **size, weight, colour, case and position** to create clear steps between levels. If two levels look similar, customers cannot tell which matters more.

### Brand-led or product-led?

Some brands lead with the brand name; others lead with the product name. Brand-led packs work when the brand is well known or the range is varied. Product-led packs help when customers search for a product type. Decide early, and be consistent across the range.

## Legibility at small sizes

### Size

Mandatory text often has legal minimum sizes, such as x-height minimums for food in the EU and UK, or minimum heights for net quantity in the US. Treat these as minimums, not targets. Where space allows, go larger.

### x-height

Two typefaces at the same point size can look very different in size because of their **x-height**, the height of lowercase letters. Typefaces with larger x-heights are easier to read at small sizes. Legal minimums are often expressed as x-height, so measure it, not just the point size.

### Weight

Very light weights can disappear at small sizes, especially on uncoated or textured materials. Very heavy weights can fill in. A regular or medium weight is usually safest for small text.

### Spacing

- **Letter spacing (tracking):** a little extra tracking can help small text and all-caps text.
- **Line spacing (leading):** generous leading improves readability of blocks of text.
- **Word spacing:** avoid tightly justified text that creates uneven gaps.

### Case

Long text in **all capitals** is harder to read. Use sentence case for information and reserve capitals for short labels or headings.

### Contrast

Dark text on a light background is easiest to read. Light text on dark backgrounds, known as reversed text, needs extra size and weight to stay legible, especially in small sizes. Avoid placing text on busy images or patterns.

### Condensed type

Condensed typefaces fit more text into narrow spaces, which is useful for ingredient lists. But heavy condensing reduces legibility. Choose a typeface designed in a condensed width rather than artificially squeezing a normal one.

## Typography and print

### Materials

- **Coated board** holds fine detail well.
- **Uncoated and textured papers** absorb ink, making thin strokes thicker and fuzzier.
- **Corrugated board** and **flexographic printing** lose fine detail.
- **Films** can show ink spread or need white underprint.

Ask your printer for minimum text sizes and line weights for your material and process.

### Reversed text

White or light text knocked out of a dark background can fill in. Use bolder weights and larger sizes, and avoid very fine serifs.

### Finishes

- **Foil stamping** and **embossing** need minimum line widths and gaps; fine type can fill in or lose definition.
- **Spot UV** on small text can be hard to align.
- **Debossed type** on thick board works best at larger sizes with simple shapes.

### Curved surfaces

On bottles and jars, wide text curves out of view. Keep important words within the visible face, and check legibility on the actual container.

## Multi-language typography

If you sell in several markets, you may need text in several languages on one pack.

- **Character support:** choose typeface families that support all required characters and accents, and scripts such as Cyrillic, Greek or Arabic if needed.
- **Text expansion:** translations can be considerably longer than English, so plan space.
- **Visual separation:** use language codes, icons, colour or layout to help customers find their language.
- **Consistency:** keep hierarchy and style consistent across languages.
- **Right-to-left scripts:** Arabic and Hebrew need different layouts and typographic handling.

## Licensing

Fonts are software, and their use is governed by licences. Before using a typeface on packaging:

- check that the licence covers **commercial use** and **packaging** or **product** use
- check any limits on **volumes**, **number of products** or **users**
- check whether **logos** created with the font are permitted
- keep records of licences

Some free fonts have licences that allow commercial use; others do not. If in doubt, ask the foundry or your designer.

## Custom lettering and typefaces

Custom lettering for a brand name or a custom typeface for a whole system makes packaging highly distinctive and avoids licensing limits. It costs more and takes longer. Many brands start with licensed typefaces and invest in custom lettering for the logo or product names as they grow.

## Typography across a range

In a range, typography is one of the strongest tools for consistency. Keep the same typefaces, weights and positions for brand, product name and variant on every product, so customers learn where to look. Variants can be separated by colour or pattern while the type system stays fixed.

## Common typography mistakes

- **Too many typefaces,** making the pack look inconsistent.
- **Weak hierarchy,** with brand, product and variant at similar sizes.
- **Decorative fonts for information** text.
- **Text too small** or too thin for the material.
- **Long all-caps text.**
- **Low contrast,** especially reversed text on dark or busy backgrounds.
- **Artificially stretched or squeezed type.**
- **Unlicensed fonts.**
- **Ignoring curved surfaces** and folds.

## A typography checklist

- One or two typefaces, with defined weights for each role.
- Clear hierarchy for brand, product, variant and benefit.
- Information text legible at actual size on the real material.
- Mandatory text above legal minimums.
- Strong contrast and no text on busy backgrounds.
- Fonts licensed for packaging use.
- Language support checked for all markets.
- Finishes and reversed text checked for minimum sizes.
