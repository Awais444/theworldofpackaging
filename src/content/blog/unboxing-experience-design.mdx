For many online brands, the box is the first physical contact a customer has with them. A good unboxing can make a product feel worth more, encourage people to share it and bring them back. A bad one, with a crushed box, rattling product or a mess of plastic filler, undoes much of the work your marketing did to win the sale. This guide explains how to plan an unboxing experience step by step, choose the right packaging and extras, and keep it practical, affordable and sustainable.

## What unboxing experience design is

Unboxing experience design is the planning of what a customer **sees, touches, reads and does**, in order, from the moment the parcel arrives to the moment the product is in their hands. It covers:

- the **outer box** and how it looks on arrival
- the **opening mechanism**
- the **first reveal** inside
- how the **product is presented** and held
- any **extras**, such as cards, samples or accessories
- what happens to the **packaging afterwards**

The aim is not to add as much as possible, but to design a sequence that feels intentional and fits your brand.

## Start with the journey, not the box

Before choosing materials, map the journey.

### 1. Arrival

The parcel arrives, often after rough handling. It should look tidy, closed and undamaged. Branding on the outside is optional: some brands want recognition at the door, others prefer discreet packaging for privacy or security.

### 2. Opening

How does the customer get in? A tear strip, a tuck flap, a ribbon pull or tape all create different feelings. Ease matters: if people need scissors or a knife, the moment becomes a chore and there is a risk of damaging the product.

### 3. First reveal

What do they see when the lid opens? This is where many brands place their biggest impact: a printed inside lid, a message, tissue paper with a sticker seal, or the product itself.

### 4. The product

The product should be **held securely** and **presented clearly**, usually in an insert. The way it sits, facing up, centred and easy to lift out, says a lot about quality.

### 5. Extras

Instructions, thank-you cards, samples or accessories. Each should have a purpose and a place.

### 6. Afterwards

Can the box be reused, recycled or kept? A rigid box that customers keep for storage extends the brand's presence at home.

## Choosing the packaging

### Printed mailer boxes

Corrugated mailer boxes, usually in E-flute or B-flute board, are the workhorse of e-commerce. They are strong, relatively affordable and can be printed inside and out. A well-designed mailer can deliver a strong unboxing on its own, especially with **inside printing**.

**Best for:** most e-commerce products, subscriptions, apparel, beauty and accessories.

### Rigid gift boxes

Rigid boxes made from thick greyboard wrapped in printed paper feel solid and premium. Magnetic closure, lid and base, and drawer styles all create a satisfying opening. Rigid boxes usually need an outer shipping box for e-commerce.

**Best for:** premium products, jewellery, fragrance, electronics, gift sets and PR kits.

### Two-stage unboxing

A plain or lightly printed shipping box protects a rigid gift box inside. The outer box takes the knocks; the inner box delivers the experience. This approach suits premium products and gifts.

### Collapsible rigid boxes

Collapsible rigid boxes look and feel like standard rigid boxes but ship and store flat, then fold into shape. They cut freight and storage volume, which matters if your packaging is produced abroad.

## Inserts: the most underrated part

An insert holds the product in place and presents it. It is often the difference between a box that feels premium and one that feels cheap.

Common insert materials:

- **Card inserts:** cut and folded board, often printed. Recyclable with the box and cost-effective.
- **EVA foam:** dense foam cut to the product's shape. Excellent protection and a premium look, but harder to recycle.
- **Molded pulp:** made from recycled paper fibres. Good protection and recyclable.
- **Velvet or flocked board:** a soft, luxurious finish for jewellery and fragrance.

## Extras that add value

The best extras are **useful, personal or delightful**, not just more stuff:

- A **short thank-you message,** printed or handwritten.
- **Clear instructions** or a QR code to a how-to video.
- A **sample** of another product.
- A **discount code** for a repeat purchase or referral.
- **Branded tissue paper** with a sticker seal.
- A **story card** about how the product was made.

Each extra adds cost and packing time, so test whether customers value it.

## Making it shareable

Many customers share unboxings on social media. To encourage it:

- **Make the first reveal photogenic,** with a clean, simple composition.
- **Use inside printing** for a message or pattern that shows in photos.
- **Include your handle or hashtag** subtly.
- **Make the product the hero,** not the packaging.

## Keeping costs under control

A memorable unboxing does not have to be expensive. Some practical ways to control cost:

- **Spend on what customers notice:** inside printing, a good insert and a message often beat expensive outer finishes.
- **Right-size the box** to reduce material, void fill and shipping costs.
- **Standardise sizes** across products where possible.
- **Use one base box** with changeable inserts or sleeves for different products or seasons.
- **Consider packing time:** complex sequences take longer to pack, which adds labour cost.

## Making it sustainable

Customers increasingly notice wasteful packaging. To keep unboxing sustainable:

- **Right-size the box** so there is little empty space.
- **Avoid plastic fillers** and bubble wrap where paper-based alternatives work.
- **Use mono-material packaging** where possible, such as card inserts in a card box.
- **Make extras useful** or skip them.
- **Tell customers how to dispose of the packaging.**
- **Use certified or recycled materials** and make only claims you can support.

## Testing before you launch

Before ordering in volume:

1. **Make a physical sample** of the full sequence with your real product.
2. **Ship it** to yourself and a few others through your normal courier.
3. **Film the unboxing** and watch for awkward moments.
4. **Ask customers** what they noticed and what they would change.
5. **Time the packing** to understand labour cost.

## Unboxing ideas by product type

Different products call for different sequences. Some starting points:

- **Beauty and skincare:** a printed mailer with a message inside the lid, products standing upright in a card insert, and a short card explaining the routine order.
- **Jewellery:** a small rigid box with a velvet or flocked insert, inside a protective mailer, with a care card.
- **Apparel:** a mailer with tissue paper and a sticker seal, the garment folded neatly with the label visible, and a returns card that is easy to find.
- **Electronics:** a rigid or heavy card box with a fitted insert, the device presented first, then cables and accessories in a separate compartment, and quick-start instructions on top.
- **Food and drink gifts:** a rigid box or sturdy carton with dividers, products arranged so the selection is visible at once, and a card describing each item.
- **Subscriptions:** a consistent base mailer, a changing insert card or sleeve each month, and a clear list of what is inside.

## Common unboxing mistakes

- **Oversized boxes** with products sliding around and lots of filler.
- **Too much tape** or packaging that needs a knife to open.
- **Plastic everywhere:** bags, bubble wrap and plastic fillers that undercut a premium feel.
- **Extras with no purpose,** such as generic flyers.
- **A beautiful inner box crushed** because the outer box was too weak.
- **No returns information,** leaving customers to search for it.
